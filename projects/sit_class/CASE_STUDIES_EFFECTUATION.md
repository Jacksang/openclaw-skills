## Case Study 1: Dyson — Affordable Loss

### What Happened (Sourced Facts)

- James Dyson spent **5 years** building **5,127 prototypes** to perfect his bagless cyclonic vacuum cleaner.
  - *Source: Wikipedia — James Dyson*  
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Dyson
  - *Source: Entrepreneur — "How 5,127 Failures Resulted in Sir James Dyson Becoming a Billionaire Inventor"*  
    https://www.entrepreneur.com/leadership/how-5127-failures-resulted-in-sir-james-dyson-becoming-a/500127
  - *Source: New York Magazine — "James Dyson on the 5,126 Vacuums That Didn't Work and the One That Finally Did"*  
    https://nymag.com/vindicated/2016/11/james-dyson-on-5-126-vacuums-that-didnt-work-and-1-that-did.html

- He funded development **entirely through personal resources**: his wife's income as an art teacher and a mortgage on their home.
  - *Source: Wikipedia — James Dyson* (same as above)

- He accumulated **over $1 million in personal debt** during the process.
  - *Source: Fast Company — "From nearly $1 million in debt to a household name"*  
    https://www.fastcompany.com/90681619/from-nearly-1-million-in-debt-to-a-household-name-james-dyson-dishes-on-his-biggest-hits-and-misses
  - *Source: Entrepreneur — "James Dyson Created His 'Mad' Vacuum Idea While $1M in Debt"*  
    https://www.entrepreneur.com/business-news/james-dyson-created-his-mad-vacuum-idea-while-1m-in-debt/495139

- Every major manufacturer rejected his design because it would cannibalize their bag-replacement revenue.
  - *Source: Wikipedia — James Dyson* (same as above)

### Effectuation Lens (Analysis)

> *"Viewed through Sarasvathy's effectuation framework, Dyson's approach exemplifies the **Affordable Loss** principle. Rather than seeking external investors or loans, he committed only what he and his family could afford to lose — his wife's salary, the equity in their home, and his own time. He was $1M in personal debt before making a single sale, yet this was his self-defined ceiling of acceptable risk. He didn't predict whether the market would accept the idea; he controlled what he could lose."*

## Case Study 2: Airbnb — Affordable Loss

### What Happened (Sourced Facts)

- In 2008, Airbnb founders Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia were **tens of thousands of dollars in personal credit card debt** funding the company.
  - *Source: Y Combinator — "The Airbnbs"*  
    https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/the-airbnbs/
    > *"They had a binder full of credit cards they'd maxed out."*
  - *Source: CNBC — "How the co-founder of Airbnb went from $25,000 in credit card debt"*  
    https://www.cnbc.com/2017/06/30/airbnb-ceo-went-from-25000-in-debt-to-running-a-30-billion-company.html
  - *Source: WIRED — "Airbnb's Surprising Path to Y Combinator"*  
    https://www.wired.com/2017/02/airbnbs-surprising-path-to-y-combinator/
    > *"Chesky... had racked up $20,000 in credit card debt each."*

- To stay alive, they designed and sold **$30,000 worth of novelty cereal** ("Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's") at $40/box during the 2008 presidential election.
  - *Source: Fortune — "Airbnb used a $40 cereal box to get its first investment"*  
    https://fortune.com/2023/04/19/airbnb-ceo-cereal-box-investors-changed-everything-billion-dollar-company/
    > *"We made $30,000 selling collectable breakfast cereal. And this is actually how we funded the company." — Brian Chesky*
  - *Source: Yahoo Finance — "How Airbnb Sold $30,000 In Obama, McCain Cereal"*  
    https://finance.yahoo.com/news/airbnb-sold-30-000-obama-204034867.html

- Their initial product was **their own apartment** — they rented out air mattresses in their living room to conference attendees.
  - *Source: Wikipedia — Airbnb*  
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airbnb
  - *Source: Business Insider / multiple interviews*

### Effectuation Lens (Analysis)

> *"Airbnb's early funding strategy is a textbook example of **Affordable Loss**. Without external investment or bank loans, the founders risked only what they could personally afford to lose — maxing out their own credit cards and using their own apartment as the product. When that wasn't enough, they didn't go to VCs; they created a cereal side-hustle that funded operations. They controlled their downside rather than predicting or betting on future success."*

## Case Study 3: Grab — Affordable Loss (with qualification)

### What Happened (Sourced Facts)

- Anthony Tan and Tan Hooi Ling won **US$25,000 (second prize)** at Harvard Business School's New Venture Competition in July 2011.
  - *Source: NLB Singapore — "Grab"*  
    https://reference.nlb.gov.sg/launch/stories/grab/

- They launched MyTeksi using **the prize money + Tan's personal savings**.
  - *Source: Wikipedia — Grab Holdings*  
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grab_(company)
    > *"Started with an initial grant of US$25,000 from Harvard Business School and Tan's personal capital."*
  - *Source: HBS Entrepreneurship — "Anthony Tan, MBA 2011"*  
    https://www.hbs.edu/entrepreneurship/founders-investors/anthony-tan

- Tan chose **not to join his family's Tan Chong automotive empire** and charted his own path, despite family displeasure.
  - *Source: NLB Singapore — "Grab"* (same as above)
  - *Source: Reuters — "From Harvard to Nasdaq listing"*  
    https://www.reuters.com/business/harvard-nasdaq-listing-grab-ceos-ride-worlds-biggest-spac-deal-2021-04-14/

### Effectuation Lens (Analysis)

> *"Through the effectuation framework, Grab's founding can be seen as reflecting **Affordable Loss**. Tan did not draw on his family's substantial automotive wealth — he and his co-founder risked only the $25K prize money and Tan's personal savings, money they could afford to lose. Additionally, he walked away from a guaranteed role in his family's business, a form of opportunity cost he was willing to bear. However, this interpretation is the author's analytical reading; Tan did not explicitly describe his actions using the affordable loss framework."*
